A hypothesis on the new world order

About two weeks ago, GPT 5.5 disproved the Erdös's unit distance conjecture. The reaction to this news was mixed - the result is impressive, but the implication is terrifying: AI is now better than humans in some of the most intellectually demanding domains we have. In the next couple of days a flurry of results dropped, either directly assisted or inspired by the results provided by GPT 5.5. This trend will surely continue - the toothpaste is out of the tube, so to speak. The elephant in the room is enormous - what is there for humans to do in knowledge work, science, and other intellectual disciplines? The intelligence we've built is starting to beat our best scientists, mathematics at their own game, and there is no sign this curve slowing down.

Its still early, but I want to advance a perspective from watching the industry most embroiled by AI today: software engineering. AI adoption in software has been jagged - there has been an enormous push to adopt this new intelligence in part driven by AI's propensity to produce prototypes and oneshot applications that no human engineer could ever write by hand. At the same time, it has met resistance from developers who see it struggle in complex, large codebases. The sense of the dissenters I think is well summarized in Mario Zechner's blog post: Slow the Fuck Down. He summarizes the experience of writing software with agents as follows:

You have zero fucking idea what's going on because you delegated all your agency to your agents. You let them run free, and they are merchants of complexity. They have seen many bad architectural decisions in their training data and throughout their RL training. You have told them to architect your application. Guess what the result is?

An immense amount of complexity, an amalgam of terrible cargo cult "industry best practices", that you didn't rein in before it was too late.

There's a paradox where for a local step of development (a new feature or bug fix), AI does it better than a human developer often both in speed and quality. However, the aggregate result becomes messy - very quickly humans stop understanding the piece of software, and then slightly later AI runs out of context and keels over. Software engineering as it stands now is about walking the tight rope - builds as fast as possible, without eventually drowning in entropy. This has even inspired some new organizational structures to codify this tradeoff between speed and sustainability - Dan Shipper, CEO of Every has suggested software teams need two people - a pirate and an architect, one designed for speed, and the other for sustainability: https://x.com/danshipper/status/2043819933675450455.

I suspect this pattern will generalize. If you want to go fast, use AI. If you want to go far, hire humans. Going far and fast is a complicated dance between natural and artificial intelligence that we haven't learned the steps to yet. So how does this relate to the Erdös problems? I think these discoveries are the sign that the mechanics of mathematical and scientific discovery are starting to be the domain of artificial intelligence. However, removing people from these programmes eliminates the essential element of the scientific enterprise - sustained advancement towards the most crucial scientific problems. If we remove people from scientific programs, we end up where purely AI built software lives today - great for demos and small to medium products, but nowhere near sufficient to create the things innovative companies operating at scale create. Sustainable advancement - if the trajectory of software has been any guide - is that rapid discovery and experimentation must be supplemented with deep human understanding that demand simplicity and combat entropy. Up to now, human creativity has been dedicated to generation new ideas. I think the mountain ahead of human creativity shifts from discovery to distillation - we are now in possession of tools that allow us to try many more ideas. The game is now about tackling the more fundamental questions and synthesize deeper ideas than we ever have before.
